Landscape

Real tricopter tilt-rotor VTOLs, across the hobby, industry, and research

This section is deliberately separate from the build manual and Research & Principles, which are both about this build. Landscape surveys the aircraft type broadly — real, documented tricopter tilt-rotor designs from other builders, companies, and researchers — so this project's own choices don't get mistaken for the only way to build one, or for universal recommendations.

Every entry here is a real, sourced design — no invented specs, no fabricated citations. Where a spec or claim couldn't be independently confirmed, the entry says so rather than guessing.

What counts as "tricopter tilt-rotor" here

This is a specific, narrow configuration, and it's easy to miscategorize: two wingtip motors that tilt between vertical (hover) and horizontal (cruise), plus one fixed motor (typically at the tail) that only runs in hover, for yaw authority and extra lift. Searching for "tilt-rotor tricopter" or "tri-rotor VTOL" turns up a lot of designs that sound similar but aren't this:

  • Tailsitters — the whole airframe pitches 90° between hover and cruise; no individual motors tilt at all.
  • Tiltwings — the whole wing (not just the motor/nacelle) rotates; a real design found during this survey (KAIST's "Tri-Copter UAV with Individually Tilted Main Wings") has "tricopter" in its own name but is a tiltwing, not a tilt-rotor.
  • Quadplanes — four or more fixed hover motors plus a separate fixed forward-cruise motor; no tilt mechanism anywhere.
  • 2-motor (V-22-style) tilt-rotors and all-3-motor-tilt designs — both real configurations, but different ones from the 2-tilt-plus-1-fixed layout this project and every entry below actually uses.

Every design on this page was checked against that specific definition before being included. A few candidates that looked promising in a first search pass turned out, on closer reading, to be one of the categories above (or simply unverifiable) — they're left out rather than stretched to fit.
