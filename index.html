This page is a working manual, not a one-time reading. Each phase below has a checklist — check items off as you go and your progress is saved in this browser (nothing is sent anywhere; it's stored locally, so it'll persist here on this device but won't follow you to another one). Phases are meant to overlap, not run strictly in order — you'll circle back to research while you're building, and that's normal for a first aircraft.

Budget assumed below is flexible ($800+) over a multi-semester timeline, and the plan assumes you're starting from limited hands-on RC/electronics experience, so Phase 00 front-loads the fundamentals you'll lean on for the rest of the build.

Want to actually understand why each of these choices works? See the Research & Principles section — the aerodynamics, propulsion physics, control theory, and structural principles behind this build, written with real citations.

Decision 1 · before anything else

Tilt-rotor tricopter, not a tailsitter

"VTOL tricopter" covers two genuinely different aircraft. A tailsitter is a fixed-wing plane that rests on its tail and pitches its whole airframe 90° between hover and cruise — mechanically simple (no tilt servos) but hard to control through transition, and awkward for anything that needs to stay level, like a camera. A tilt-rotor tricopter keeps the fuselage level the whole time: two motors on the wingtips rotate from vertical (hover) to horizontal (cruise) while a third, fixed motor at the tail handles yaw and extra lift in hover. Recommendation: build the tilt-rotor tricopter first. It has real, documented precedent to build from and is the more forgiving path for a first VTOL.

Recommended

Tilt-rotor tricopter

2 wingtip motors tilt 0–90° on servos; 1 fixed rear motor for hover yaw. Fixed wing + elevons for cruise control.

Precedent: MiniHawk VTOL (open-source, 3D-printed, this exact layout) and the commercial E-flite Convergence, which PX4 already has a reference config for.

Later / stretch

Tailsitter

Whole airframe rotates. No tilt mechanism to build, but transition control and payload orientation are both harder to get right.

Worth building as a second, simpler aircraft once you've flown the tilt-rotor and want to compare control strategies — see ArduPilot's tailsitter guide.

00

Foundations

Semester 1~4–6 wks
▸

Before designing anything, get fluent in the vocabulary and the physical building blocks: what a brushless motor/ESC/LiPo system actually does, how radio control links work, and what a flight controller is doing 400+ times a second. Skipping this is the single biggest cause of a first scratch-build never leaving the ground.

Coursework tie-in circuits & intro electronics courses cover the Ohm's-law-level stuff you'll use constantly when sizing wire gauge, fuses, and current draw.

02

Configuration

Semester 1~2–3 wks
▸

Lock the layout on paper before touching CAD. This is where "Decision 2" (flight controller) and the airframe's rough geometry get fixed, because changing them later means re-cutting parts you already built.

Decision 2

dRehmFlight vs. ArduPilot QuadPlane

dRehmFlight (runs on a $25 Teensy 4.0) is source code you read and edit directly — no GCS, no black-box autopilot logic. Best if the goal is to actually understand every line of the control loop, which fits a mechatronics track well. ArduPilot QuadPlane/tilt-rotor runs on a Pixhawk-class board, is far more capable (GPS waypoints, automatic transition, failsafes) but you're tuning parameters in an existing framework rather than writing the control code. Recommendation: start with dRehmFlight for v1 to learn the control theory hands-on, then consider migrating to ArduPilot for v2 once you want autonomy features.

Key resources
  • dRehmFlight on GitHub
  • ArduPilot QuadPlane frame setup (source docs)
  • Fusion for students & educators (free)
03

Analysis & sizing

Semester 2~3–5 wks
▸

Turn the sketch into numbers: an airfoil and wing that generate enough lift at your target cruise speed, a propulsion system sized to both hover and cruise thrust requirements (these are very different loading cases), and a weight-and-balance budget you'll hold yourself to during fabrication.

Coursework tie-in statics, mechanics of materials, and any fluids/aero elective map directly onto wing loading, spar sizing, and drag estimation here.
Key resources
  • XFLR5 — free airfoil/wing analysis · intro tutorial (PDF)
  • eCalc xcopterCalc — multirotor/hover propulsion sizing · propCalc for fixed-wing cruise sizing
04

Components & BOM

Semester 2~2 wks + shipping
▸

Convert Phase 03's sizing numbers into an actual parts order. Buy once, buy correctly — a mis-sized motor or the wrong ESC firmware costs a re-order and a week of shipping later.

Where people buy this stuff
  • GetFPV and RaceDayQuads — motors, ESCs, FCs, batteries; both stock fixed-wing/VTOL-specific parts, not just racing-drone gear.
  • Local hobby shops for servos/glue/pushrods when you don't want to wait on shipping.
05

Fabrication

Semester 2~4–8 wks
▸

Build the airframe. Whatever method you chose in Phase 02, build in this order: structural skeleton first, then the tilt mechanism (it's the part most likely to need a second attempt), then skinning/finishing last.

Coursework tie-in manufacturing processes and CAD/CAM courses feed straight into slicer settings, tolerancing for press-fits, and design-for-assembly decisions here.
06

Electronics & firmware

Semester 3~2–3 wks
▸

Wire it, flash it, and prove every actuator moves the right way before the aircraft is ever off the bench. This phase ends with a full pre-flight bench test, not a flight.

Coursework tie-in this is the mechatronics core: embedded systems/microcontrollers for the firmware side, sensors & instrumentation for IMU/airspeed integration, controls for the PID loop you're about to tune in Phase 07.
07

Ground test & tune

Semester 3~2–3 wks
▸

Everything in this phase happens with the aircraft tied down or held. The goal is a hover-mode PID that's stable enough to trust in free air, and a pre-flight checklist you'll actually follow every single time.

08

Flight test

Semester 3–4ongoing
▸

Test incrementally — every step here should be boring if the previous step went well. The transition between hover and cruise is where most first-attempt VTOLs get damaged, so it gets the most caution.

Key resource
  • ArduPilot VTOL tuning guide — written for tailsitters but the transition-tuning philosophy (altitude margin, abort criteria, incremental testing) applies directly.
09

Iterate & document

Ongoing—
▸

A first VTOL is a prototype, not a final product. This phase is where the project turns from "I built a thing" into a portfolio piece and, if you want, a competition entry.

⚠ Safety notes worth re-reading before every flight season

LiPo batteries can and do catch fire when damaged, over-discharged, or charged incorrectly — always charge in a fireproof bag/container, away from anything flammable. Never stand in the arc of a spinning propeller, including during bench testing. AMA membership is worth getting before your first flight test: it's inexpensive, typically includes liability insurance coverage while flying at AMA-sanctioned sites, and gets you access to an actual runway/field instead of a random park. Fly line-of-sight, under 400 ft, and clear of people, per FAA recreational rules — the tilt-rotor transition especially should always be tested with plenty of altitude and open space around you.
